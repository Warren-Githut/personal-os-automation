# STE100 at 80% — Vietnamese application notes

ASD-STE100 is an international standard for **technical documentation in
English**, first released in 1986 by what is now ASD (the AeroSpace and Defense
Industries Association of Europe). European airlines asked for it: their
maintenance staff were mostly not native English speakers, and a misread
instruction on an aircraft can kill people. It has been free to download since
Issue 6 (2013); the current edition is Issue 9 (January 2025).

The standard has two parts:

- **Part 1 — writing rules.** 53 rules across 9 sections covering words, verbs,
  sentences, procedures, descriptive text, safety instructions, punctuation and
  general writing practice.
- **Part 2 — dictionary.** Roughly 900 approved words, each restricted to one
  meaning and one part of speech, plus roughly 1,200 words to avoid with
  suggested replacements.

Organisations may add their own approved technical nouns and verbs on top of
the base dictionary — this is the "terminology allowance" and it is how a
domain keeps its own jargon without breaking the rules.

## The rules that actually change how prose reads

These seven carry most of the readability gain. The rest of the 53 are
refinements of these.

### 1. Approved words only (Rule 1.1)

Every word must be approved, in its approved meaning and its approved part of
speech. Each approved word maps to **exactly one** meaning — context is not
allowed to disambiguate a word with several dictionary senses.

*English example.* `check` is approved as a **noun** ("do a check"), not as a
verb ("check the lights"). `about` is approved only with the meaning
"concerned with", not "approximately" — `approximately` is separately approved.

*Vietnamese.* There is no Vietnamese edition of the dictionary, so there is no
such thing as a Vietnamese "approved word list". Apply the **principle**: one
word, one meaning, per document. If a term means two different things in the
same document, rename one of them.

### 2. Plainest correct word

When English has synonyms, STE picks one and excludes the rest — it uses
`start`, never `begin`, `commence`, `initiate` or `originate`.

*Vietnamese.* Prefer the plain Vietnamese word over a loanword when it is
accurate. "Bắt đầu" over "khởi động" when the object is a meeting; the reverse
for a machine. Accuracy beats the rule.

### 3. Approved verb for an action, not a noun from that verb (Rule 3.7)

*Vietnamese.* Prefer the verb. "Kiểm tra" over "sự kiểm tra" when an action is
meant.

### 4. No phrasal verbs (Rule 9.3)

Do not build a verb out of a verb plus a preposition. The meaning is not
predictable from the parts, which is exactly what breaks non-native readers and
translation systems.

*English.* Not "look after". Say "maintain".

*Vietnamese.* "Lo" + noun is borderline: "lo việc đặt bàn" is readable, but
"lo lắng" is a real word with a different meaning — do not use it where "lo" was
meant as "take care of".

### 5. Verb forms — six permitted, no compound tenses

Permitted: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future,
and past participle used only as an adjective. Banned: present perfect, past
perfect, both progressive forms.

*English.* "We have received" is banned. "We received" is fine. "The panel has
been removed" is banned. "The system is checking the input" is banned.

*Vietnamese.* Vietnamese tense is mostly expressed by adverbial words, not
morphology, so the ban rarely bites. But the **perfect aspect** ("đã ... rồi",
"đang ... đang") carries the same ambiguity: it hides whether the action is
finished and whether it still holds. For procedures, use the plain present or
the imperative and let the step order carry the timing.

### 6. One instruction per sentence; ≤20 words (procedures), ≤25 (descriptions)

Do not shorten by deleting parts — dropping the verb, subject or article to hit
the word count creates ambiguity instead of clarity. The standard explicitly
warns against this.

*Vietnamese.* Vietnamese counts words differently from English: multi-syllable
syllables are single words. A 20-word Vietnamese sentence is roughly a
30-word English one in speaking time. 20 is therefore a reasonable ceiling, and
15 is a better target for a step Bố reads while holding a tray.

Passive voice is allowed only in descriptive text, and only when the actor is
genuinely unknown or irrelevant to the reader.

## Vietnamese-specific notes

- **Do not claim "ASD-STE100 compliant".** There is no Vietnamese edition. Say
  "STE100-style", or "80% STE100", and name which rules you actually applied.
- **Numbers are never rounded and never paraphrased** (vault A3). "128.4M" stays
  "128.4M" in every format, including narration.
- **Confidence tags survive translation** (SOUL §4). `[HIGH]`/`[MOD]`/`[LOW]`
  are markers, not prose — they stay in the label.
- **Bullet form is not STE100, but it is the right form for Warren's quick
  reads.** `warren-style-embedding` §Visual-Learner Output Format asks for
  `•` bullets with section icons because long unbroken prose loses him. When
  STE100 discipline and Warren's format preference collide on a Telegram or
  summary deliverable, bullets win — the rules were never the goal, readability
  was.

## What STE100 is not good for

It was built for aircraft maintenance instructions. It has no opinion about
argument, narrative, persuasion or teaching. Applying all 53 rules to a
recommendation memo produces clear sentences that argue nothing. Use it on
procedures, specifications and reference text — not on advice.
